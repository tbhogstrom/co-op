## 2. Key Questions Asked and Responses Given

### Question 1: "What makes this different from a partnership?"

Good question and it came fast. I explained the LLC cooperative structure — not a general partnership where everyone is jointly liable. Limited liability. One member, one vote regardless of capital. Defined exit process. Formal operating agreement drafted by an attorney. Wren said: "So it's like a union hall but for flipping houses?" I said that is actually not a bad analogy — shared governance, collective benefit, but applied to property investment instead of dispatching labor. Wren paused. "Okay, keep going."

### Question 2: "How many flips per year? Am I giving up my business?"

I explained: 2-3 flips per year to start, each requiring 2-4 weeks of electrical scope. The rest of the time, Wren runs the independent business as usual. The co-op supplements income — it does not replace it. Wren relaxed visibly (audibly). "That's important. I'm not looking to shut down my shop."

### Question 3: "What's the buy-in?"

Walked through the tiers. Wren zeroed in on Tier C — $5,000. Asked: "And at $5K I get the same vote as someone who puts in $40K?" Yes. One member, one vote. Wren said: "That's unusual. Most of the time the money talks." I explained that the labor share is where the real economics are, and that is driven by hours worked, not capital contributed. Wren listened but did not commit to a number. Said "I'd need to think about what I could put in."

### Question 4: "Who else is in this? Do I know anyone besides Copper?"

I named the founding candidates by trade: carpenter, roofer, plumber, plus Harlan as project manager and Maven as the organizer. Did not name names beyond Copper. Wren asked if Harlan was "the GC who works the east side." I confirmed — Harlan's reputation apparently precedes him, which is positive. Wren said: "If Copper and Harlan are in, there's a level of quality there."

### Question 5: "K&T — is that what you need me for?"

This came up when I described the target properties. Wren brought up K&T proactively, which is a good sign, but then said something I noted carefully: "I've done some K&T work but it's not my main thing. I do more panel upgrades and new circuits — EV chargers, heat pump hookups, that kind of thing." This is the key gap I expected. See qualifying information below.

## 4. Cooperative Fit Assessment

When I asked about multi-trade crew coordination, Wren gave a good answer: "I prefer working with people I know. If I know the plumber is going to be in the walls on Tuesday, I can plan my rough-in around that instead of showing up and finding surprises." This is practical coordination thinking — exactly what works on a rehab project.

On what appeals: "The idea that I'd have a say in which houses we buy. Right now I just show up where I'm told and deal with whatever's there. If I could look at the electrical before we commit — that changes things."

On skepticism: "I've been in the union and I've been independent. Both have problems. The union is great for protection but you don't have control. Being independent is great for control but you eat what you kill. If this is actually in between — real ownership but with a crew — I'm interested. But I want to see the numbers on paper."

That answer tells me Wren gets the co-op concept at a structural level. The dual-card experience is exactly the foundation for understanding cooperative ownership.

## 8. Calloway's Assessment

Wren is a solid electrician with strong panel upgrade skills, modern-systems expertise, and the right temperament for cooperative work. The dual-card background is a genuine asset for someone entering a cooperative structure. Copper's referral carries weight, and the existing cross-trade relationship is real.

However, the K&T gap is a problem for this specific co-op. Portland's target neighborhoods — Lents, Cully, Parkrose — are dominated by pre-1940s housing with knob-and-tube wiring. K&T remediation is not an occasional scope; it is the default electrical scope on nearly every project. Volt has 30+ K&T projects. Wren has 8-10. That is a significant difference for a founding member who will be the co-op's primary electrician.

Wren remains a viable candidate for two scenarios:
1. **If Volt does not convert.** Wren's K&T skills are adequate — not expert-level, but competent. The gap could be closed with a few projects under Harlan's coordination.
2. **As a future member (Year 1 addition).** If Volt joins as founding electrician, Wren could be recruited later when the co-op expands into properties that need more modern electrical work — EV chargers, heat pump systems, sub-panel additions. Wren's skill set becomes more valuable as the co-op's project portfolio diversifies.

For now: send the info packet, schedule the follow-up, and keep the relationship warm. Do not close the door. But Volt is the primary target for founding membership.
