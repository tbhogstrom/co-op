> "Understood. No pressure at all. If you're ever curious down the road, [Harlan / Copper] knows how to reach me. Appreciate your time."

## The Value Proposition for Electricians (3-5 minutes)

Transition into this once they express any curiosity. Lead with economics, not philosophy.

### Lead with the Money

> "Here's why we're specifically looking for an electrician. On a typical Portland rehab — let's say a 1940s bungalow in Lents or Cully — the electrical scope runs $8,000 to $20,000. Panel upgrade, K&T remediation, maybe a full rewire. Right now, if we sub that out, every dollar of that labor value leaves the co-op."

> "If you're on the team, that value stays in-house. You'd earn a labor share on every project you work — your hours, weighted at a 1.3x trade multiplier, against the total labor pool. That multiplier reflects what licensed electrical work is worth relative to general labor. On top of that, you'd earn a capital share — 30% of gross profit split proportional to your equity stake."

### Translate to Real Numbers

> "On a conservative first flip — say $40,750 gross profit — the labor pool is about $16,300. If you put in 150 hours of electrical work at 1.3x, that's 195 weighted hours out of roughly a 2,000-hour total pool. That's about 10% of the labor share — roughly $1,600 in labor distribution. Plus your capital share on top of that. And you'd draw a labor advance during the project so you're not working on spec."

> "Compare that to subcontracting the same scope: you'd bid it, do the work, collect your invoice, and that's it. No upside on the sale. No equity. No vote on the next deal."

### The Ownership Piece

> "The other thing — and this is what got [Copper / Harlan] interested — is that you're not just doing electrical for someone else's flip. You're at the table when we pick the property. You see the pro forma. You vote on whether to buy. If the deal is bad, you can say no. If the deal is good, you share in it."

> "Every member gets one vote regardless of how much capital they put in. The electrician's vote counts the same as the founder's."

## Address Common Concerns (as they come up)

Do not front-load these. Let the candidate raise them. If they don't come up on the first call, that's fine — they will on the second.

### "How much do I have to put in?"

> "Minimum buy-in is $5,000 — that's what we call Tier C, the sweat equity entry level. You can go higher if you want a larger capital share — Tier B is $10K to $25K. But $5,000 gets you in the door with full membership, full voting rights, full labor share access. That capital is equity — it's yours, it grows as the co-op succeeds, and there's a defined process to get it back if you leave."

### "What if I need to get out?"

> "Ninety days' written notice. Your capital account balance — that's your original buy-in plus any allocated profit — gets paid back within 60 days of the effective date. If the co-op is cash-tight, it can be paid over 12 months in installments. You keep any distributions you've already received. You get paid out for any completed projects you worked on. Clean exit."

> "The one restriction: no withdrawals during an active project. If there's a flip in progress, your exit date slides to 30 days after that project closes. That protects everyone — including you — from mid-project disruption."

### "Is there a non-compete?"

> "Yes, but it's narrow. You can't flip houses in the Portland metro area for 12 months after you leave. That's it. You can absolutely keep running your own electrical business, take other clients, do subcontract work for other GCs — all of that is explicitly allowed. The restriction is only on the specific activity of buying and flipping residential property in our target market. It's designed to protect the co-op's deal pipeline, not to limit your trade work."

### "How much time does this take?"

> "The co-op runs projects, not a job site every day. When a flip is active, you'd be on site for your electrical scope — could be 2-4 weeks of focused work depending on the project. Between projects, there's a monthly members' meeting and occasional governance work. We're targeting 2-3 flips per year to start. This is designed to complement your existing business, not replace it."

### "Who's already in?"

> "The founding organizer is Maven. Harlan is our GC — 20 years of Portland residential rehab. We have three trade members in vetting right now: a carpenter, a roofer, and a plumber. All licensed, all experienced, all Portland-based. You'd be the fifth founding member if this works out. The founding members set the culture and the standards — that's a real thing."

### "What if a project loses money?"

> "Losses reduce future distributions. The co-op absorbs the loss first through the reserve fund (20% of every profit gets set aside). If it's bad enough, there could be a pro-rata capital call, but your buy-in is the floor of your personal exposure. You're not personally liable for co-op debt beyond your capital account."

## Qualifying Questions (5-7 minutes)

Transition naturally. These are not an interrogation — frame them as "help me understand your background so I can see if this is a fit."

### License and Credentials

- "What Oregon electrical license do you hold — journeyman or master?"
- "Is your CCB license current? Residential endorsement?"
- "Are you current on EPA RRP certification?" [If no: "That's a hard requirement for the co-op — every member needs it because we're working on pre-1978 housing. It's a renewal course, about $275 at NW Environmental Training Center. Would that be a barrier?"]

### Experience — The Critical Scopes

- "How much K&T remediation have you done? Is that a regular part of your work, or occasional?"
  - [We need someone who has done this repeatedly. K&T is on almost every pre-1940s Portland project. If they've done fewer than 5 K&T jobs, note it as a gap.]
- "Panel upgrades — 100A to 200A residential service. How often do you do those?"
- "Have you dealt with aluminum branch wiring remediation?"
- "How comfortable are you with the Oregon Electrical Specialty Code inspection process? Any failed inspections in the last two years?"

### Business and Availability

- "How many residential jobs are you running per year right now?"
- "What does your schedule look like for the next 6 months? Would you have capacity to commit 2-4 weeks to a project starting late summer or fall?"
- "Do you have your own tools and equipment for a full residential rewire, or would you need to supplement?"

### Cooperative Fit

- "Have you ever worked on a multi-trade crew where you had to coordinate closely with other trades on the same timeline? How did that go?"
- "What appeals to you about this — if anything? What makes you skeptical?"
  - [Listen more than talk here. Their answer tells you whether this is a financial play, a values play, or not a play at all.]

## Closing the Call (2 minutes)

### If Interest Is Strong

> "This sounds like it could be a good fit. Here's what I'd suggest as a next step: I'll send you an information packet — it covers the business model, the profit-split formula, and the co-op structure. Take a week to look it through. If you have questions or want to talk further, I'm available anytime. And if you'd like to meet [Harlan / Copper] in person — they're the ones who know your work — we can set that up."

### If Interest Is Lukewarm

> "I appreciate you hearing me out. No rush on any of this. I can send you some written materials if you want to look at it on your own time — no commitment, just information. And if it's not for you, no hard feelings. We'd still love to work with you as a subcontractor on projects."

### If No Interest

> "Totally understand. Thanks for the conversation. If anything changes or you know someone else in the trade who might be interested, [Harlan / Copper] knows how to reach me."
