## 1. Vision & Mission (CFO Perspective)

### Vision

Build a self-sustaining, worker-owned cooperative that creates wealth for its members
by acquiring, renovating, and selling residential properties in the Portland metro area —
proving that construction workers don't need outside investors to capture the full value
of their labor.

### Mission

The Portland Housing Co-op exists to:

1. **Pool capital and labor** from skilled tradespeople who are currently shut out of real
   estate investment returns.
2. **Execute profitable fix-and-flip projects** with a target net ROI of ≥15% per project.
3. **Distribute profits equitably** through a transparent formula that rewards both capital
   contribution and labor.
4. **Build reserves** so the co-op can scale from 1 flip/year to 5+ within 5 years.
5. **Keep construction dollars in worker pockets** instead of flowing to outside investors,
   general contractors, and middlemen.

### What Makes This Different

In a typical flip, the money person takes 50-70% of profit and the labor gets wages.
Here, the workers *are* the investors. A carpenter who puts in $15K and 400 hours of
framing gets paid on both sides of the ledger. That's the core economic proposition.

## 2. Capitalization Target

### How Much Money Does the Co-op Need?

Short answer: **$200,000 in member equity** to safely execute the first flip.

Here's the math:

#### First-Flip Capital Stack (Conservative Scenario)

| Line Item | Amount | Notes |
|-----------|--------|-------|
| **Purchase Price** | $300,000 | Target: distressed SFR in Portland metro (Lents, Woodstock, Foster-Powell, outer SE/NE) |
| Hard Money Loan (85% LTV) | ($255,000) | Typical Portland hard money: 85% LTV, 10-12% interest, 2-3 pts origination |
| **Down Payment Required** | $45,000 | 15% of purchase price |
| **Rehab Budget** | $75,000 | Moderate rehab — kitchen, bath, flooring, paint, systems |
| Hard Money Rehab Draw (70% of rehab) | ($52,500) | Most lenders will fund 70-80% of rehab in draws |
| **Cash Needed for Rehab** | $22,500 | Member equity covers the gap |
| **Acquisition Costs** | $12,000 | Inspection, title, appraisal, loan origination (~4% of purchase) |
| **Carrying Costs (5 months)** | $18,750 | See breakdown below |
| **Selling Costs** | $28,500 | 6% of ARV ($475K) for agent commissions + closing |
| **Operating Overhead (pro-rated)** | $15,000 | Insurance, admin, tools — 5 months of year 1 overhead |
| **Contingency (15% of rehab)** | $11,250 | Things always go wrong on the first one |
| | | |
| **Total Cash Required from Co-op** | **$153,000** | |
| **Recommended Capitalization** | **$200,000** | $153K needed + $47K liquidity buffer |

#### Carrying Cost Breakdown (5-Month Hold)

| Item | Monthly | 5-Month Total |
|------|---------|---------------|
| Hard Money Interest (11% on $255K) | $2,338 | $11,688 |
| Property Taxes | $375 | $1,875 |
| Insurance (Builder's Risk) | $250 | $1,250 |
| Utilities | $300 | $1,500 |
| Misc (permits, dumpsters, etc.) | $488 | $2,438 |
| **Total** | **$3,750** | **$18,750** |

#### Why $200K and Not $153K?

Cash flow kills deals, not profit margins. The $47K buffer covers:

- **Rehab overruns** (first flip WILL have surprises)
- **Extended hold time** (what if it takes 7 months instead of 5?)
- **Slow sale** (what if it sits on market for 60 days?)
- **Unexpected costs** (permit delays, weather, material price spikes)

A co-op that runs out of cash mid-rehab is dead. The buffer is non-negotiable.

## 3. Member Buy-In Structure

### Target: 6-8 Founding Members

| Tier | Buy-In Amount | Equity Share | Target Members | Notes |
|------|---------------|--------------|----------------|-------|
| **Tier A — Full Share** | $25,000 - $50,000 | Proportional to contribution | 2-3 members | Likely members with savings or HELOC access |
| **Tier B — Working Share** | $10,000 - $24,999 | Proportional to contribution | 3-4 members | Tradespeople investing what they can |
| **Tier C — Sweat Equity Entry** | $5,000 | Proportional to contribution | 1-2 members | Low cash but high-skill members; balance via labor |

### How It Works

1. **Capital accounts are individual.** Each member's buy-in is tracked to the penny.
   Your $25K contribution is YOUR equity in the co-op.

2. **Equity is not equal — it's proportional.** A member who puts in $25K owns a larger
   capital share than a member who puts in $5K. But profit splits also weight labor
   heavily (40% of distributable profit goes proportional to hours worked). This means
   a Tier C member who works 600 hours on the rehab can out-earn a Tier A member
   who only works 200 hours.

3. **Buy-in is an investment, not a fee.** Members get their capital back (plus returns)
   before the co-op distributes profit. If the co-op dissolves, capital accounts are
   returned in full before any remaining assets are split.

4. **Additional contributions are welcome.** If a member wants to increase their buy-in
   before a project, they can. Capital accounts are adjusted accordingly.

5. **Withdrawal terms.** Members can withdraw capital with 90 days written notice,
   subject to:
   - Not during an active project (no pulling money mid-rehab)
   - Co-op must maintain minimum reserve balance after withdrawal
   - Board approval required

| Member | Role | Buy-In | % of Capital |
|--------|------|--------|--------------|
| Member A | Carpenter | $30,000 | 15.0% |
| Member B | Plumber | $25,000 | 12.5% |
| Member C | Electrician | $25,000 | 12.5% |

| Member F | Painter/Finish | $10,000 | 5.0% |

### Key Policy Decisions Needed (for Maven/Statton)

1. **Minimum buy-in floor?** I recommend $5,000. Below that, the administrative overhead
   of tracking micro-equity isn't worth it.
2. **Maximum buy-in cap?** Consider capping at $50K to prevent any single member from
   having outsized control. Co-ops work on democratic governance — one member, one vote —
   regardless of capital share.
3. **Payment plans?** Allow members to pay buy-in over 3-6 months? This increases
   accessibility but delays full capitalization.
4. **Sweat equity conversion rate?** Can a member "earn" additional equity through labor
   beyond their cash buy-in? If so, at what rate? (I'd suggest tracking this but not
   implementing until after the first flip — keep it simple.)

## 5. Risk Factors

| Risk | Impact | Mitigation |
|------|--------|------------|
| Rehab goes 30% over budget | Eats $22.5K of profit; ROI drops to ~9% | Detailed scope of work before purchase; contingency budget; experienced trades |
| Property sits 90 days on market | Additional $11.3K in carrying costs | Price competitively; stage the property; have agent lined up pre-listing |
| Hard money rates spike | Higher carrying costs | Lock rate at loan origination; keep rehab timeline tight |
| Member exits mid-project | Cash flow disruption; labor gap | 90-day withdrawal notice; cross-train members; maintain labor bench |
| Portland market correction (5-10% drop) | ARV drops $25-50K; may lose money on conservative deal | Only buy at ≤65% of ARV; build in margin of safety |
| Insurance claim / injury | Project delay; cost increase | Workers comp, GL, builder's risk all required before first day on site |
